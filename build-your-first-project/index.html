Pick the right model for the job

Click the model name next to the send button. The menu remembers your last choice.

A good daily driver for light work.

Balanced

Your standard for everyday, non-technical work.

Opus

Slower, smarter, uses more

Work through a long report or a big spreadsheet, plan something with many steps.

Your standard for complex, technical work and analysis.

Fable

Slowest, "smartest", burns through usage

Only for the toughest problems. If Opus can't get there, give Fable a shot.

Keep an eye on your usage in Settings, then Usage.

  • Most people never touch this menu. It's the easiest upgrade in Claude.
  • Match the model to the job: Haiku for a quick fix, Sonnet for most of your day, Opus for more complex work, deeper analysis and harder thinking, Fable only for the toughest problems.
  • For example: Sonnet for a client follow-up or a meeting summary. Opus for a month of data, a messy spreadsheet, or planning a quarterly review. Haiku for "fix this sentence".
  • Bigger models are slower and use more of your allowance. Settings, then Usage, shows where you stand.
  • The menu remembers your last pick. If you switched to Opus for one big job, switch back after.
  • "What should my default be?" Sonnet.

What is "effort"?

Effort is how much Claude thinks before it answers. More thinking is slower and uses more; less is faster and lighter. Find it in the model menu, under Effort.

Low

Quick, routine asks.

Medium

Routine tasks. Faster, and keeps usage lower.

High

The best balance of quality and speed.

Extra high

Deeper thinking for hard, multi-step problems. Slower than High.

Max

Rarely. Very slow, and it can overthink.

  • Too slow or long-winded? Drop to Medium or Low.
  • Careful with Max, even for technical work. Like people, Claude can overthink, and the result can get worse.
  • Effort is how long Claude thinks before it answers. It sits in the same menu as the model.
  • My rule: set it to High and leave it. That's the best balance of quality and speed.
  • Drop to Medium or Low when it's slow or long-winded on simple things, like rewording an email.
  • Shallow answer on a hard problem? Try Extra high before you rewrite the prompt. More thinking often fixes it.
  • Max is rarely worth it, even for technical work. It's very slow, and like people, Claude can overthink a problem and land somewhere worse.
  • Curious how it got an answer? Click "Thinking" above the reply.

A good prompt has three parts

  1. Set the stage. Who you are, your goal, and the background.
  2. Define the task. Exactly what you want Claude to do.
  3. Specify the rules. Length, tone, format, and an example if you have one.

Brief it like a smart coworker on their first day.

Weak

"Write an invite email for our next AI lunch-and-learn."

Better

I run a monthly lunch-and-learn on AI tools for about 30 coworkers, most of them new to AI. Write the invite email for next month's session on Claude Projects. Under 120 words, friendly and plain, no hype. Start with what they'll be able to do afterward, then the date and the meeting link.

  • This structure comes straight from Anthropic's Claude 101 course: set the stage, define the task, specify the rules.
  • Try it: send the weak prompt in one chat and the better one in another, then compare. What did Claude have to guess in the first one?
  • "Prompt engineering" sounds technical. For everyday work it just means briefing Claude like a smart coworker on their first day: capable, but they don't know your clients, your audience or your standards.
  • Setting the stage is the part most people skip. Who you are, who it's for and why it matters change the whole answer.
  • Rules cover length, tone and format. The strongest rule is an example: if you have one you liked, paste it.
  • The weak one isn't wrong, it's generic. Claude filled every gap with the most average guess.
  • Hold on to this. In Part 2, a Project saves this whole brief so you never retype it.

If it's...Do this
Too genericAdd who it's for, your role and your limits.
The wrong lengthSay the length: "under 120 words", "3 bullets".
The wrong formatShow, don't just tell: paste an example.
The wrong toneDescribe it plainly ("friendly, not salesy") and add an example.
Confident but wrongCheck it. Ask where it got that, or what it's unsure about.

First drafts are starting points

Reply with specific feedback: what to change, and why. "Under 120 words" works better than "make it shorter".

  • These five come from Claude 101. Almost every disappointing answer is one of them.
  • Try it on the weak result from the last slide. Reply with one specific fix: "It's for about 30 coworkers who are new to AI. Keep it under 120 words and lead with what they'll be able to do."
  • The pattern is to say exactly what to change. "Under 120 words" beats "shorter". "Friendly, not salesy" beats "better tone".
  • Showing beats telling. If the format is wrong, paste an example of the format you want.
  • Confident but wrong is the one to watch. Claude can sound sure and still be wrong, especially with names, numbers and dates. Ask "where did you get that?" or "what are you unsure about?"
  • If a reply goes sideways, you can edit your last message and resend it instead of piling corrections on top.

Every chat fills up

Everything in a chat takes up room in Claude's working memory, its context. Back-and-forth is fine, but past a point Claude gets worse at using it all.

Compacting

A full chat gets squeezed into a summary ("organizing its thoughts"). Details get lost.

New chat

Back to a clean start, in the smart zone. Bring only what matters with you.

  • Every new chat is like an "innie" from Severance: it walks in knowing nothing about your other chats, only what's in the room. That's a feature: a clean start every time.
  • Everything in a chat fills Claude's working memory, its context: your messages, its replies, your files.
  • Matt Pocock at AI Hero calls the early part the smart zone and the late part the dumb zone. Normal back-and-forth is fine. Once a chat is most of the way full, Claude gets worse at using what's in it.
  • Big files and very long chats fill it fastest. A 100-page PDF takes far more room than a page of notes.
  • When a chat is nearly full, Claude compacts it: it summarizes the older messages and keeps going, and you'll see "organizing its thoughts". Summaries drop details, and long chats use more of your allowance.
  • The habit: long chat, Claude forgetting what you told it, or "organizing its thoughts"? Start a new chat and bring only what matters. That's exactly what the brain dump does.
  • One thing does carry into every new chat, and that's memory.

Set up memory so Claude knows you

With memory on, Claude keeps context from your chats, like your role, your work and how you like things written, and brings it into new chats.

  • Make sure you have memory turned on.
  • Look through what it remembers. Delete what isn't relevant. To add something, tell Claude in any chat: "Remember that..."
  • To keep one chat out of memory: start a new chat, click + in the message box, and switch Memory off before you send your first message.

Where to find it

  1. Click your name or initials (bottom left), then Settings.
  2. Open Memory.
  3. Turn on Generate memory from chats.
  4. Open Topics to review and edit.
  • Memory is the quickest win in Claude. One switch, and new chats already know your role, your work and how you like things written.
  • Depending on your plan, it may be off until you turn it on.
  • It's saved as Topics you can read, edit or delete. Worth a two-minute cleanup: old projects, wrong details, anything you don't want following you around.
  • You can also tell it directly in any chat, for example: "Remember that I write to clients in a friendly, direct tone."
  • Want one chat kept out of memory? Switch Memory off from the + menu before your first message.
  • Memory knows you, but loosely. It won't hold your exact template or rules. That's what Part 2 is for.
  • Give it about 3 minutes. The steps are in the kit.

Part 2: Build your first Project

Every new chat starts from zero.

So you re-explain your role, your client and your format, and paste the same files again. A Project is a workspace where every chat already knows your instructions and your files.

  • Here's the gap memory leaves: it remembers you, but loosely. It won't hold your template, your exact format or your rules.
  • Remember the 3-part prompt? Imagine never typing the stage and the rules again. That's a Project: the brief and your best example, saved once.
  • Ask the room: who has pasted the same background into Claude more than twice this week?

You keep re-explaining? Make a Project.

  • Make one Project per thing you make over and over. A single Project for everything gives generic answers.
  • Brainstorming something new? Use a fresh chat instead.

Prompt

A one-off request that's gone when the chat ends.

Project

A standing setup for one kind of work. That's today.

Skill

A saved how-to Claude can reuse anywhere. A good next step.

  • The test is simple: do you keep re-explaining the same background, or re-uploading the same files? Make a Project.
  • One Project per recurring thing. "Monthly client recap" is a Project. "My work" isn't: a catch-all gives generic answers because Claude can't tell which rules apply.
  • Brainstorming something brand new is still a fresh chat.
  • Prompt, Project, Skill: a prompt is one-off, a Project is a standing setup for one kind of work, and a Skill is a saved how-to Claude can use anywhere. Anthropic puts it as "projects store knowledge, skills perform tasks."
  • Ideas: client recaps, call follow-ups, weekly updates, month-end commentary, answers to the questions new hires always ask.

A Project = instructions + files

Instructions

How Claude should do the job, including the steps and rules.

Rules go here, for example:

  • "Lead with the total, then 3 bullets."
  • "Keep it under 150 words."
  • "If a number is missing, ask. Never guess."

Files

Reference material Claude reads in every chat. It copies the style and format of your examples.

References go here, for example:

  • A past recap you were happy with
  • Your template
  • A product or process one-pager

Every new chat in the Project starts with both.

  • Two parts: instructions are how Claude should do the job; files are what it should know and copy from.
  • Rules go in the instructions and references in the files. A rule buried inside a PDF is easy for Claude to miss.
  • Claude copies an example better than it follows a description. Your best example is your most valuable file.
  • Every new chat in the Project starts with both. But chats don't see each other, so a fix you argue out in one chat is gone in the next. Fix the instructions instead; Sprint 3 practices that.
  • Don't count on Claude reading the Project's name or description. Anything it needs goes in the instructions or files.
  • With memory on, each Project keeps its own memory, marked "Only you". People you share with don't see it.
  • Files can be up to 30MB each, and Claude can search a big Project. Still, a few clear files beat a pile.

  • Name it after what it makes, like "Monthly client recap", not "My stuff". You'll have several Projects soon.
  • "Use a folder" ties the Project to your computer, and then you can't share it. Skip it. If the page says local projects are only on this computer, make a new one without it.

Instructions: how to build them

  1. Context: who you are, what it's for, who reads it
  2. Process: the steps, in order
  3. Tone and format: taken from your example
  4. Specific requirements: 3-5 rules, each with a reason
  5. When I give you: what you'll paste in each time, and what Claude does with it. For example: "When I give you my weekly bullets, write my update."

Every Project can benefit from these two rules

"If information is missing, say so and ask. Never invent numbers or facts."

"Mark anything you inferred."

  • This is what the Project Starter hands you in the next sprint, so you'll recognize it.
  • It's Anthropic's structure for Project instructions: Context, Process, Tone and format, Specific requirements, plus a "When I give you" line.
  • "When I give you" is the shortcut. Once it's set, you paste this week's notes and the Project already knows the job.
  • Each rule has a reason. Claude follows a rule better when it knows why, for example "Under 150 words, because they read it on their phone."
  • The two rules on the right make everything checkable: missing info gets flagged and guesses get marked. You'll lean on them in Sprint 4.
  • You don't need these exact headings. It's a solid starting point, not the only way.

  • Nobody should start from a blank box. Claude guesses and you correct, which is much faster than writing from scratch.

  • "You decide" is a real answer. You can change anything later.
  • Expect 6 to 8 questions. If time gets tight: "You decide on the rest and write it now."

Fix the instructions, not the chat

New chat, your notes, ask in your own words

Check it

Check it prompt: accuracy, and a match to your example

Teach it

Add one sentence to the instructions

Try again

New chat, same notes

Better = closer to your example's format and tone, and nothing hallucinated.

  • Use it, check it, teach it. That's the whole loop, and it's how a Project gets good.
  • A correction in a chat dies with that chat; a sentence in the instructions applies to every chat after.
  • A new chat doesn't remember the old one, so paste or attach the notes again.
  • Not better? Check it once more and add a second sentence. Specific beats general: "Start with the date and time" works better than "be clearer".
  • Don't over-check. Once or twice is enough. Keep asking Claude for problems and it starts inventing them.
Sprint 3

Use it, check it, teach it

  1. Use it: new chat in your Project, Chat selected. Paste your notes or attach your file with +, and ask in your own words: "Write my follow-up from these notes."
  2. Check it: paste the Check it prompt from the kit in the same chat. It flags anything wrong or off from your example, and writes one sentence to fix it.
  3. Teach it: copy that sentence. Project page, + next to Instructions, add it at the end, Save instructions.
  4. Try again in a new chat with the same notes.

Done early? New chat, same notes, plus "Treat [one number] as missing." Claude should flag the gap, not invent a figure.

Your name is on it

Claude drafts it, but you own it. Read through it and verify it before you send it. It should be concise, make sense, and sound like you, not like AI. If you couldn't defend it as if you wrote it yourself, it isn't ready.

  1. Read it. All of it, before anyone else has to.
  2. Understand it. If you couldn't explain it, don't send it.
  3. Validate it. Facts, names and numbers match your source. Nothing hallucinated: check what Claude marked as inferred.
  4. Make it yours. Your words, your tone. Cut the filler and the jargon. You'd sign it.
  • Claude drafts it, and you own it. Anything you send is yours, exactly as if you wrote it.
  • Don't be the person who replies to a coworker with "Claude said:" and a wall of text. They could have asked Claude themselves, and faster.
  • AI writing is often long, plausible and full of jargon. Reading it, checking it and putting it in your own words is the value you add.
  • The test: could you defend every line if someone asked "where did this come from?"
  • Your Project's two rules help. Missing info gets flagged and guesses get marked, so you know exactly where to look.
  • You'll run these 4 checks on your real output next.
Sprint 4

Use it for real

  1. Pick something you need this week. New chat in your Project, give it the input, ask in your own words.
  2. Run the 4 checks: read it, understand it, validate it, make it yours. Edit until you'd sign it.
  3. Optional: Share (top right of the Project page) with a teammate, Can view. They see your instructions and files, not your chats.
11:00

  • This is the point of the whole thing: one real piece of work you'll actually use this week.
  • No real input today? Use a recent one, or try the "treat a number as missing" test.
  • Sharing is optional. Can view means they can chat with it. Can edit means they can change your instructions and files, which you usually don't want.
